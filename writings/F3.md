# F3: Problem Framing

## Domain

Interest exploration for middle schoolers. Around the world, middle schoolers (students ages 11 to 14 years old) begin to explore potential future paths for their life. Most students are bounded by the experiences they already have had and the experiences of those around them, especially their family members. They also might have preconceived notions about certain interests and jobs that could deter them without allowing the student to fully explore the option. Additionally, students ages 11 to 14 begin to develop their own hobbies and interests. Both of these developments are motivated by the growth of individuals into preteens and teenagers who have more autonomy and personal identity. The primary stakeholders in this domain are middle school students looking to explore potential interests. Teachers are also a stakeholder in this domain, as they likely want to help students explore their interests and find their passions. Parents are another stakeholder in this domain. Parents want to help their children find their passions and explore the opportunities that the world has for them.

## Bad situations

- **Limited exposure to new interests.**
  - George is a middle school student who wants to discover what he enjoys but doesn't know where to start. He tends to stick to familiar activities, limiting his opportunities to discover new interests. Even when he already knows what he likes, he may not be aware of the different possibilities within that field. For example, George loves movies but only thinks of acting or directing, without realizing he could also explore screenwriting, cinematography, sound design, or film editing. As a result, he may miss out on activities he would genuinely enjoy simply because he doesn't know they exist.
- **Social pressure narrows choices.**
  - Middle schoolers may choose activities based on what their peers consider "cool" rather than what they personally find interesting. Social expectations and stereotypes can discourage students from trying unfamiliar activities. For example, George's classmates believe that "cool guys play football" and figure skating is "for girls." Even though George might enjoy figure skating, he never considers trying it because of how others might react. Without a space to explore independently, he may continue choosing activities based on social expectations rather than his own curiosity.

## Evidence

### Student Interest

According to a 2022 study, 85% of middle schoolers in the United States are already thinking about future careers and 87% are interested in ways to match their skills and interests to potential careers ([Edutopia](https://www.edutopia.org/article/8-ways-to-help-middle-schoolers-imagine-their-future-careers)).

### Student Behaviors

The Association for Career and Technical Education (2018) identified middle school as a core period for career awareness, career knowledge accumulation, and the preliminary establishment of career goals ([frontiers](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/391568173_Improving_career_readiness_in_middle_school_students_a_systematic_review_of_intervention_approaches/link/682eb4768a76251f22e526b6/download)). Despite middle school being shown as a core period for career development, studies show that most middle school students have unclear career perceptions, misjudge the skill demands of jobs, and experience a disconnect between education and career. Students begin to rule out unfamiliar career opportunities as early as middle school, meaning that providing students with opportunities to explore different options than what surrounds them in middle school can prevent students from ruling out options early ([ECS](https://www.ecs.org/state-of-middle-school-career-exploration/)). Earlier career exposure can increase students' likelihood of successfully transitioning into postsecondary options that are increasingly necessary for good jobs.

### Challenges to Career Exploration

Some of the main challenges that students face in career and interest exploration in middle school are limited funding, pressure on core academic time, and overburdened school counselors. These challenges limit the abilities of students to explore new opportunities and feel supported throughout the process.

### Interest Exploration and Identity Development

A literature review on adolescent identity development identifies three types of exploration: discovering unfamiliar activities, exploring existing interests more deeply, and reflecting on those experiences. These are important ways adolescents can learn more about themselves and what they enjoy. This supports our focus on giving middle school students opportunities to explore both new interests and different paths within familiar ones. ([Source](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-018-9457-3))

### Social Influence

Peers can also influence which activities students choose to participate in. A study of 421 middle school students examined the relationships between friendships, afterschool science participation, and students' identification with science. The findings highlight how interests and social relationships can be connected, reinforcing the importance of giving students opportunities to explore beyond their immediate social circles. ([Source](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11554726/))

## Workarounds and comparables

**Offline workarounds.** Currently, students can explore new interests through school clubs, extracurricular activities, summer camps, community programs, or with the help of their parents and friends. These opportunities allow students to actually try different activities, but they are often limited by availability, cost, transportation, and scheduling. Students also usually need to have some idea of what they want to try before joining. Social pressure can make this even harder, as students may choose activities their friends participate in rather than exploring something unfamiliar.

**Existing digital solutions.** Several platforms also help students explore new interests:

**Outschool** offers online classes in a wide range of subjects, but students still need to choose what they want to learn, and many classes require payment.

**YouScience** uses aptitude assessments to help students identify strengths and explore career paths. However, it focuses more on assessing abilities than giving students opportunities to experiment with different interests.

**DIY.org** provides creative challenges that kids can try themselves, but it does not offer the same personalized, story-based exploration journey we envision.

While these solutions offer opportunities for exploration, students often still need to know what they want to try beforehand. We want to make that first step easier by introducing unfamiliar activities through relatable stories and allowing students to experiment with them through small challenges. Unlike a list of courses or activities, Ask Jordan also lets students reflect on their experiences and explore related interests over time. Our goal is to complement existing opportunities, such as school clubs and community programs, rather than replace them.

## Solution sketch

We propose **Ask Jordan**, an AI-powered interactive storytelling app that helps middle school students discover and explore new interests through personalized stories and small practical challenges.

When students first join, they share some information about themselves, including their current interests, things they have tried before, what they are curious about, and what opportunities or resources they have available. Based on this information, the app suggests several activities they could explore, but students can also choose something completely different.

The app then generates a short, personalized story chapter in which a character explores the selected activity. Each chapter introduces what the activity involves, how to get started, and some basic concepts and terminology. Students can make choices throughout the story and try a small challenge related to the activity.

We chose storytelling because simply giving students a list of activities might not help them understand what those activities are actually like. Stories can introduce unfamiliar fields through relatable characters and situations, while mini-challenges allow students to try something themselves without committing to a class, club, or expensive equipment. We want the experience to feel like an interactive adventure rather than another school assignment.

After each chapter, students reflect on what they liked or disliked, what they found interesting or challenging, and whether they would like to explore further. Based on these reflections, the app suggests new directions. Students can continue exploring the same interest, try something related, or move on to something completely different.

For example, George might start with his interest in soccer and choose to explore sports photography. After reading a chapter and trying a small photography challenge, he might realize that he enjoys the creative side of sports and decide to explore filmmaking next. Within filmmaking, he could then discover other possibilities, such as screenwriting, directing, or sound design.

The app also includes an interest map where students can look back at what they have explored and which activities they have actually tried. This gives them a way to track their discoveries without grades, rankings, or comparisons with other students.

Our goal is not to identify students' hidden talents or push them toward specific careers, but to help them explore different possibilities and discover what they genuinely enjoy. AI recommendations are meant to introduce new options, not limit students to what the system thinks they should pursue.

## User Journey

George is a middle school student who enjoys soccer and watching movies, but he has never really explored activities outside of what he already knows. He wants to try something new but doesn't know where to start.

**1. Creating a profile.** George signs up for Ask Jordan and creates his profile. He shares his current interests, including soccer and movies, along with some information about what he has tried before and what resources he has available.

**2. Discovering new possibilities.** Based on his profile, Ask Jordan suggests several activities, including sports photography, filmmaking, and creative writing. George is surprised by sports photography because he has never considered that there are creative activities connected to soccer. He decides to give it a try.

**3. Exploring through a story.** The app generates a short, interactive story chapter about a character trying sports photography for the first time. Through the story, George learns what sports photographers do, how they capture movement, and some basic photography techniques. He also makes choices throughout the story that shape what happens next.

**4. Trying a mini-challenge.** At the end of the chapter, George receives a small challenge: try taking a few action photos using a phone or another available camera. He can complete it at his own pace, without grades or pressure to perform well.

**5. Reflecting on the experience.** After the chapter, George shares what he liked and disliked. He realizes that he enjoyed thinking about camera angles and capturing movement, even though some of his photos didn't turn out as expected.

**6. Choosing what comes next.** Ask Jordan updates George's exploration history and interest map, distinguishing between reading about an activity and actually trying it. It then suggests several directions, including continuing with sports photography, exploring filmmaking, or trying an unrelated activity. George chooses filmmaking.

As George continues exploring, he discovers that filmmaking includes many different activities, such as directing, screenwriting, cinematography, and sound design. He can explore these paths individually, try small challenges, and decide which ones interest him.

Over time, George builds a collection of experiences that helps him understand what he enjoys. Rather than telling him what he should be good at or what career he should pursue, Ask Jordan gives him opportunities to discover new interests and make his own choices.

## Design summary

The user signs in through Authenticating and Sessioning, then creates an Account with their name, a short description, and some starting interests. These interests are copied into an InterestCollection. InterestGeneration suggests more interests based on what the user has accepted or rejected, and the user chooses one to explore. StoryGeneration then uses their profile description to write a story about that interest, with a max of 3 chapters. Each chapter starts an ExplorationTracking record, and the third chapter marks the exploration as complete. This creates a Reflecting journal where the user can write about their experience. AvatarCustomizing lets the user equip items they have been granted. None of the concepts stores talent labels, career recommendations, or information about other students’ activity.

Reflections are private to the user who writes them. Parents, schools, and other users have no action that lets them read the journal. This also avoids introducing a parent view that could make a child less comfortable writing honestly. The user decides which suggested interest to pursue, and rejecting a suggestion does not remove an interest already in their collection. Stories end after three chapters, giving each session a clear stopping point. Suggestions draw on the user’s own accepts and rejects. The design includes no ads or actions for selling or sharing profile information.

Several parts of the design still need to be worked out. InterestGeneration currently has no action for recording the interests entered in Account, so there is no way to use those interests to generate the first suggestions. ExplorationTracking also creates a separate record for each chapter, but the reaction that marks a story as finished looks up only one exploration. These two parts need to agree on what an exploration represents.

We also need to decide what it means to have “tried” an interest. The third chapter includes an activity as text, but the exploration is marked complete as soon as that chapter exists, before the user writes about doing the activity. At the moment, completion therefore reflects reaching the end of the story, rather than necessarily doing the activity. Another unresolved issue is the narrowing risk identified in the ethics table: nothing currently requires suggestions to go beyond the interests the user has already named.

A few other features are described but not yet connected to the design. AvatarCustomizing.grant is never called because no action returns an item to grant. The interest map shown in the journey would be a view of the InterestCollection, but that view has not been specified. Finally, the ethics table calls for parental consent before storing a profile for a child under 13, but no concept currently asks for that consent.

The story and the suggestions both depend on a model returning text in the shape the actions expect. When a call fails or comes back unusable, StoryGeneration returns a story we wrote ahead of time, and the user can still choose an interest they already entered, read it, and write a reflection. Alpha is built on those prepared stories. Beta keeps them available and adds live generation next to them, so a failure at the demo does not take the rest of the path down.

If the interests on the profile are still not reaching InterestGeneration by November 30, we do not hold beta for suggestions. The user keeps choosing among the interests they typed, which is the path alpha already runs.

Avatar rewards depend on some action returning an item that `grant` can record. The interest map is a view of the collection and the completed explorations, so it does not get its own update when an exploration finishes. If that item never appears, avatar rewards stay out of beta, and the user still finishes a story and a reflection.

If we have not built a way to ask a parent for consent by the user testing stage, the person who walks through the app is 13 or older, and we do not store a profile for a younger child.

An exploration is the whole story, and the user marks it complete. Alpha only starts that record. If we are still unsure how completion should work at the first checkpoint, alpha does not need the exploration to be completed, so the first running version can ship while that decision is still open.
