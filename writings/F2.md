# F2: Problem Framing

## Domain

Interest exploration for middle schoolers. Around the world, middle schoolers (students ages 11 to 14 years old) begin to explore potential future paths for their life. Most students are bounded by the experiences they already have had and the experiences of those around them, especially their family members. They also might have preconceived notions about certain interests and jobs that could deter them without allowing the student to fully explore the option. Additionally, students ages 11 to 14 begin to develop their own hobbies and interests. Both of these developments are motivated by the growth of individuals into preteens and teenagers who have more autonomy and personal identity. The primary stakeholders in this domain are middle school students looking to explore potential interests. Teachers are also a stakeholder in this domain, as they likely want to help students explore their interests and find their passions. Parents are another stakeholder in this domain. Parents want to help their children find their passions and explore the opportunities that the world has for them.

## Bad situations

- **Limited exposure to new interests.**
  - A middle schooler named George wants to discover what he enjoys but doesn't know where to start. He tends to stick to familiar activities or follow his peers, this limits his opportunities to discover new interests. For example, George might have a natural talent for volleyball but never considers trying it simply because all his classmates play soccer.
- **Classroom pace doesn't fit every student.**
  - Students learn at different speeds, but a standardized classroom pace can make it difficult for them to recognize their strengths. A student who struggles to keep up may conclude they are "not academically gifted," while a student who is ready to move ahead may become disengaged. For example, Regina takes longer than her classmates to memorize maths formulas. This makes her believe that she isn't talented at STEM. However, her ability to grasp complex concepts quickly and develop a deep understanding of them goes unnoticed because the classroom emphasizes memorization over conceptual understanding.
- **Social pressure narrows choices.**
  - Middle schoolers may choose activities based on what their peers consider "cool" rather than their genuine interests or abilities. Social expectations and stereotypes can discourage them from exploring unfamiliar activities and discovering hidden talents. For example, George's classmates believe that "cool guys play football" and that figure skating is "for girls." As a result, George never considers trying figure skating, even though his natural agility and quickness might make him particularly good at it. Without exposure to the sport or relatable role models, he may never discover this potential interest.

## Evidence

### Student Interest
According to a 2022 study, 85% of middle schoolers in the United States are already thinking about future careers and 87% are interested in ways to match their skills and interests to potential careers ([Edutopia](https://www.edutopia.org/article/8-ways-to-help-middle-schoolers-imagine-their-future-careers)).

### Student Behaviors
The Association for Career and Technical Education (2018) identified middle school as a core period for career awareness, career knowledge accumulation, and the preliminary establishment of career goals ([frontiers](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/391568173_Improving_career_readiness_in_middle_school_students_a_systematic_review_of_intervention_approaches/link/682eb4768a76251f22e526b6/download)). Despite middle school being shown as a core period for career development, studies show that most middle school students have unclear career perceptions, misjudge the skill demands of jobs, and experience a disconnect between education and career. Students begin to rule out unfamiliar career opportunities as early as middle school, meaning that providing students with opportunities to explore different options than what surrounds them in middle school can prevent students from ruling out options early ([ECS](https://www.ecs.org/state-of-middle-school-career-exploration/)). Earlier career exposure can increase students' likelihood of successfully transitioning into postsecondary options that are increasingly necessary for good jobs.

### Challenges to Career Exploration
Some of the main challenges that students face in career and interest exploration in middle school are limited funding, pressure on core academic time, and overburdened school counselors. These challenges limit the abilities of students to explore new opportunities and feel supported throughout the process.



## Workarounds and comparables

**Offline workarounds.** Currently, students can explore new interests through school clubs, extracurricular activities, summer camps, community programs, or with the help of their parents and friends. These opportunities allow students to actually try different activities, but they are often limited by availability, cost, transportation, and scheduling. Students also usually need to have some idea of what they want to try before joining. Social pressure can make this even harder, as students may choose activities their friends participate in rather than exploring something unfamiliar.

**Existing digital solutions.** Several platforms also help students explore new interests:

**Outschool** offers online classes in a wide range of subjects, but students still need to choose what they want to learn, and many classes require payment.

**YouScience** uses aptitude assessments to help students identify strengths and explore career paths. However, it focuses more on assessing abilities than giving students opportunities to experiment with different interests.

**DIY.org** provides creative challenges that kids can try themselves, but it does not offer the same personalized, story-based exploration journey we envision.

While these approaches provide opportunities for discovery, they don't always help students take the first step toward something completely unfamiliar. We want to make exploration more accessible and less intimidating by letting students learn about and try different activities through personalized stories and small challenges, before deciding whether they want to pursue them further.

## Solution sketch

We propose **Ask Jordan**, an AI-powered interactive storytelling app that helps middle school students discover and explore new interests through personalized stories and small practical challenges.

When students first join, they share some information about themselves, including their current interests, things they have tried before, what they are curious about, and what opportunities or resources they have available. Based on this information, the app suggests several activities they could explore, and students choose what interests them.

The app then generates a personalized story chapter in which a character explores the selected activity. The chapter introduces what the activity involves, how to get started, and some of its basic concepts and terminology. It also includes a small challenge that allows students to try something related to the activity themselves, at their own pace.

After completing a chapter, students reflect on their experience: what they liked or disliked, what they found interesting or difficult, and whether they would like to explore the topic further. Based on their reflections, the app offers new choices. Students can continue exploring the same activity, try something related, or move on to something completely different.

For example, George might start with his interest in soccer and choose to explore sports photography. After reading a chapter and trying a small photography challenge, he might realize he enjoys the creative side of sports and decide to explore filmmaking next.

We also plan to include a personal journal where students can look back at their experiences and interests. Additional features, such as a parent view or seeing what other students are exploring, could be added later.

Our goal is not to tell students what they are talented at, but to give them opportunities to explore different fields, including those they might not encounter in school, and discover what they genuinely enjoy without grades, time pressure, or social expectations.

## Ethical analysis

| Stakeholder                                                                  | Short-term impact                                                              | Long-term impact                                                                                 | Actions                                                                                                         |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Middle-school users**                                                      | Discover activities; risk exposing private reflections; increased screen-time. | Broader interests, or AI suggestions that repeatedly narrow their options, provide a role-model. | Keep journals private; let children choose suggestions; make in-app experience minimal and not overstimulating. |
| **Parents/guardians**                                                        | Can support exploration; access to journals may inhibit honest reflection.     | Stronger support, or possible reduced child trust.                                               | Show children exactly what parents can see; test if summaries suffice *(law restrictions)*.                     |
| **Children facing cost or access barriers**                                  | Suggested activities may be impractical.                                       | Unequal access to opportunities may persist.                                                     | Add free, at-home options.                                                                                      |
| **Developers, investors, shareholders** *(e.g. extracurricular club owners)* | Personalization creates value but entails responsibility for child data.       | Engagement or revenue incentives may conflict with children’s interests.                         | Prohibit ads, data sales, and model training + audit outputs.                                                   |
| **Schools** *(if added later)*                                               | Gain an exploration tool.                                                      | Wider access, disclosure of children’s interests, more data for putting together clubs.          | Assess school use separately; offer a non-app alternative, let parents and children know what schools can see.  |
